The way I work with agents has changed dramatically since the summer, and I think it's finally time to sit down and write about it.
Before I get started: this was heavily inspired by [Thorsten's latest post about it](https://x.com/thorstenball/status/2114386050638336082?a=ntwTZikRT1ZWxWKAJEs&s=20). Make sure to read it!
Here's the key takeaway: **be more ambitious, dare to ask**. Models can one-shot features and work alone for at least half an hour; they can design A/B tests to verify the changes you had them make to prompts and skills; and they can comfortably navigate across complex and messy data platforms. You should update how you use them to match these new capabilities. And stop babysitting them: ==ask more==.

## Long-running tasks

When I say "ask more", I mean it quite literally: my prompts got longer and vaguer at the same time. I use voice mode, I ramble, and I can also change my mind halfway through a sentence. What I give the agent is a goal, a rough plan and pointers to where things live, never precise file paths. I rarely list the steps to take, though I am still explicit about when I want it to fan out the exploration to subagents or to do a round of review.
Last week I had to gather requirements for the front end of a proof of concept, a web app that shows how a few families of models are trained and what they produce. My first message was a 700-word voice note. It told the agent to look in one repository, clone another one and "look in [a colleague's] branch, there should be a web app there". Then: "spawn many subagents, don't work yourself, you don't work, you don't modify the code". Each subagent had to build a variant of the UI, "only UI, only HTML, mocked data", once another subagent had worked out the user stories. On the data: "try to use the real table names. Then the data can be fake, it doesn't even have to be much". And what I expected: "I come back, reopen, look at each one separately, annotate them and then give you another round of feedback."
The vague part is only the beginning, though. Most of the later iterations took a couple of minutes, a few took up to half an hour, and the long prompts were precise about how to finish: move the mock-ups to a given folder, open a single pull request with a screenshot of each, say I'm betting on four of the eight versions and why, and "don't say anything more than that". I also said which model should do the mechanical parts, because "we're burning credits for nothing, give precise instructions".
The orchestrator and nine subagents worked for 34 minutes and wrote 55 files of research (user stories, a content spec, notes on the repositories) before coming back with four questions. My reply, in full: "q1: I don't know, in dev we have a hash / q2: who cares for now / q3: I don't know / q4: we don't know". That was enough for another 13 minutes and the first eight mock-ups. Over five days and 38 of my messages, 34 subagents read 328 files across four repositories (the main one has about 300 commits on 16 branches) and produced eight interactive mock-ups, the user stories and a guide for interviewing stakeholders. I never pointed it to a file or a table.
I never instructed the agent about how to gather context: I just gave it the outcome and where to start. Unprompted, the agent also wrote a content spec (the structure every prototype should follow) and a context guide with what it had learned about the data catalog: about 80 tables, some with hundreds of millions of rows and one with roughly four billion transactions a year. It never queried them: it worked it all out from the schemas in the code and the docs. The eight subagents that produced the PoCs picked up the work straight away from those context files, with no further exploration.
To be clear: I wasn't in LGTM mode. I reviewed all the artifacts and had the agent make significant changes in every round. The first version was far from perfect in terms of style, though the agent nailed my requirements exactly: each prototype was unique and yet built on the same fundamental outline I wanted it to have. It helped me diverge and explore. I then narrowed the prototypes down to four, had the agent trim the interview guide, dropped the user stories artifact, and kept the content spec and context report.
This week I wanted to compare how two teams train and score the same kind of models. One repository I barely named, the other lives on a different Git server, and all I said was "the name of the repo is pretty obvious". The agent fetched both, went through their remote branches to find each team's latest work and compared them in throwaway worktrees. The rest of the 250-word voice note listed what to compare (the loss, the cross-validation strategy, holdout sets, scoring metrics, pros and cons) and asked it to flag "very big mistakes". The orchestrator and four subagents worked for 22 minutes and came back with an interactive HTML report. My follow-ups were about the writing style, not the content: I just dropped a link in the chat to Shreya Shankar's [Five Principles for Generating Readable AI Text in Bulk | Full Stack Data Lab](https://fsdatalab.github.io/blog/agent-text-at-scale/).
The first pass is not always right, and I still steer. But the expensive part, finding out where things are and how they fit together, is something I can now hand over with a paragraph of half-formed thoughts.

## Cloud agents

Agents in the cloud are becoming the default everywhere. Amp has had it for a while, with [Orbs](https://ampcode.com/what-are-orbs). Claude and ChatGPT have it too: after all, [OpenAI acquired Ona](https://ona.com/stories/ona-joins-openai), formerly Gitpod, precisely for this. Companies like [Paradev](https://paradev.com/) (formerly Superset), [Capy](https://capy.ai/) and [Conductor](https://www.conductor.build/) (acquired by Meta) offer cloud environments for their workspace-like products, building on primitives like [durable objects](https://earendil.com/posts/pi-durable-on-cloudflare/).
This unlocks many use cases, the simplest one being that I can start a session from my phone while on the metro, and check the results once I hit my desk at the office. Besides, cloud sessions run in sandboxes, which helps if you are concerned about agents touching files on your personal machine (which still needs to happen, from time to time).
This is nothing new: people have been doing that for at least a year, back when Mac Minis did not cost a fortune. But now it's gone mainstream, and the biggest players include it in their subscriptions at no extra cost.
What gets me excited is **where it's all headed**, but more on that at the end.
The only downside I see is when your cloud agents don't run the same versions of programs as your local machine: this happened too many times with Claude threads. Just prompt the agent to set up a script that does that 🙃 (Nix users are gonna love this.)

## Farewell, TUI

Everyone who knows me knows how much I love(d) working from the terminal, using ~~obscure~~ well-designed CLIs. I can say I went without an IDE well before it was mainstream: Neovim has _always_ been my favourite editor. In fact, I started writing this post inside Neovim, while Claude was drafting a bespoke blog post editor. Well, things changed.
Last year I wrote that "coding agents belong to the terminal". It was not wrong at the time, but it's not how agents should be used now.
I need a GUI for the same reason I used Neovim back when we mostly wrote code by hand: _speed_. Desktop apps make it _easier_ to work with multiple projects and multiple threads; there's an integrated browser to manage pull requests or review HTML artifacts; ChatGPT has a great annotation tool to provide precise feedback about web pages. And don't forget about plugins, AKA MCP apps! The first time I used the Miro plugin inside ChatGPT I wasn't expecting to see a fully functioning canvas pop up in the middle of the session.
If you look at it, this isn't just a matter of "I don't want to juggle the browser and the terminal". ==Agents are moving to where work happens==. Companies run agents via Slack. Matt Pocock has agents run inside channels in his private Discord server. I don't mean to dunk on the terminal or TUIs: ==it's just that we are developing better interfaces that match the new and improved capabilities of LLMs, and you might not want to end up stuck in the 2026 equivalent of tab completion==. We moved past that. Keep trying out new things!

## HTML, HTML everywhere

Markdown artifacts are done for. They were never the right abstraction to begin with. They were hard to read, _when_ we read them. There's a reason the web is not just plain text: we grok concepts more easily when they are visual, better yet **interactive**.
Now I ask agents to build bespoke, one-off HTML files: mostly for interactive explainers and planning documents _à la_ [Plannotator](https://plannotator.ai/), with clear "Yes/No/Later" buttons and more ergonomic ways to give feedback or make precise edits. HTML files enable more back and forth and more collaborative design.
[Amp dropped plan mode](https://x.com/beyang/status/2001150592480313425?a=eYrL9UC-hCI96TZGND0&s=20) and it's not the default anymore in Claude too, but planning isn't dead. Asking the agent to include previews, interactive diagrams, or short screencasts helps me _choose_ and iterate on the design better and faster. It's much easier to reason about three mock-ups and pick one, rather than reading about them in the abstract. You can throw away these files afterwards, or attach them to the pull request description to keep a history of the feature (I don't like committing them).

## One orchestrator, individual project threads

Models have become smart enough not to get lost. I use Claude projects, with a long-running coordinator session to manage something as broad as "this blog". Thread cache hit ratios rarely fall below 98%. I don't know when, or how, Claude does auto compaction. I don't think I have memories turned on: the coordinator thread remembers the preferences that surface in the threads.
At work, I reproduced a similar setup: I am currently running GitHub Copilot's Opus 5.5 on OpenCode Desktop and I have been working on a 400k-token-long orchestrator session since Friday. No performance drops.
To be fair, a colleague of mine cleverly pointed out that this might entail higher costs: for example, when I get back to the session after the lunch break, the whole session needs to be written to the cache again. However, I don't see how starting a new session from zero can be cheaper. What I like the most is that I don't have to juggle many sessions.
I am not in love with Claude's main thread. I find it mostly routes my messages to existing and working threads. With OpenCode Desktop I _feel_ I could customise this behaviour more easily. To be honest, I don't think it affects the results enough to be worth fixing now.
On the bright side: I don't need AGENTS.md files, nor do I use any skills besides custom ones that instruct Claude on the format I want it to use for pull request descriptions. Sometimes I run /grill-me when I want planning to be more thorough.

## Code reviews: intent, artifacts and tests

I changed my view about code reviews, too. I don't think we need to validate the code we write as much as we did in the past. To be fair: there have always been pull requests where code was never meant to be reviewed - think of exploratory data analysis.
Most of the time, when it comes to my personal projects, I don't really care about the code as much as the outcome. I have never reviewed a single line of code for this blog, though I routinely ask what could be done to improve the codebase's coherence. It's also a nice way to learn a bit of TypeScript in the meantime.
What I do care about is for the agent to provide an informative description. Above all, I want to see visual proof of the changes. [visual-pr](https://github.com/humanlayer/skills#visual-pr) from HumanLayer helps. When it's possible, seeing a screenshot or screencast helps a lot to narrow down the scope of the review. At first, I had the agent upload a screen recording of the changes to the blog. It was very expensive in terms of tokens, so now I just check the preview build and have the agent perform the fixes: it's simply faster.
This first step is about confirming whether what was built matches my intent. Then I focus on the how, and how it was tested. I want the agent to tell me _why_ something was done and not _what_ (in the PR description it's just noise): the trade-offs, the alternatives and why they were discarded. This is more of a recap and a proof of the planning session, but some decisions only come up during implementation, so that's where I review them. To be honest, this is the main reason I still open pull requests: they are where I record the choices and the alternatives we discarded. If I didn't need a place to keep track of decisions, I would probably use them a lot less.
These two steps give me ==guidance== on how to perform the review: requirements first, architecture second, and finally the code. It's much easier to perform a _layered_ review than it's ever been.
At this point I can decide whether I need to review the code at all, and how closely. This is the "tiered review" approach [Matt Pocock presented at AI Engineer in Paris](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LlgiOCmFG_w): I'm more lenient when a change has a low blast radius and is a two-way door, i.e. it can be easily reverted. If I am writing a migration for the production database, I read every pixel of it. Demos also serve as proof that something works, more so than tests, which agents are still bad at writing and are my favourite entry point for code review. The most successful prompt I use for refactoring goes a bit like this:

> Look for tangled tests that need heavy mocking or reach into internals instead of going through a clean interface, and use them as a signal of tightly coupled abstractions or weak encapsulation.

In this way, the model finds a lot of coupled interfaces and suggests a better architecture that follows Uncle Bob's Good Ol' SOLID Principles. In [this refactoring session for the blog](https://github.com/baggiponte/blog/pull/75), the model discovered a lot of tests were reading the same mocked JSON file and split the interface in two.
In general, I am okay with the first implementation being sketchy and with having another round of refactoring. If you force the agent to write clean code from the start, it might lose sight of the goal, but most importantly it will eat up tokens and take more time, so your iterations will be slower.

## What's next

I don't think the points I discussed are particularly novel or groundbreaking: they have simply become mainstream. There are three things I'd like to explore next.
The first is loops. I'd like to set up automations (the infamous "loop engineering"), for example to trigger code reviews automatically. At work, I am mostly looking forward to "goal engineering", i.e. letting the agent build a harness to hill-climb against a well-defined metric, such as the performance of a machine learning model. My experience is still limited to one session, when I asked Claude (in pi) "Can we bench nvim startuptime now?" and "if we can see something like a flamegraph from that bench it'd be super useful".
The start was quite rocky: a subagent timed the startup with hyperfine and promptly hung for three hours on a quoting bug in one of its own commands, until I asked how it was going and the orchestrator killed it. After that, it took 16 minutes to build two flame graphs, one from LuaJIT's sampling profiler and one from the `--startuptime` log, and a review page with eight suggestions ranked by impact, each with a yes/no button. The verdict: my config was already lean (😏), but the suggestions were legit. I was still blown away that it worked out on its own how to profile the code, then built the flame graphs and suggested and ranked the improvements. The tools for performance analysis have been great for a while; what's remarkable is that it handled all of this complexity by itself.
The second is interfaces. TUIs might be over, but terminals are turning into something else. [Herdr](https://herdr.dev/) and [Rex](https://www.superlogical.com/rex/docs/installation), a new kind of multiplexer by Superlogical, the company founded by Mitchell Hashimoto (of HashiCorp and Ghostty), look a lot like terminals _now_, but their team said they don't expect people to use Rex with terminal panes _only_. Meanwhile, apps are going plugin-first: Pi came first, then OpenCode V2, and DeepSeek Harness brought the idea to desktop apps. You can now ask OpenCode Desktop, and even Claude (!), to mod its own UI. I'm genuinely fascinated by building bespoke interfaces this way.
The third is collaboration, which is where cloud agents are heading. Amp Orbs can already be shared: a teammate can send me a thread to collaborate on or finish. People work on shared threads via Slack, and products like [Buzz](https://buzz.xyz/) and [Intent](https://intentapp.dev/), which replaces chat sessions with "spaces", are built to be multiplayer. Still, I think the most important unsolved problem is allowing a team to collaborate and record (architectural) choices as a project evolves, so I am looking forward to [Chopin](https://githubnext.com/projects/chopin/) by Maggie Appleton's team at GitHub Next becoming GA. The blog post is worth reading, and you can listen to the extended version in this episode of [The Pragmatic Engineer](https://open.spotify.com/episode/1gdAYoMg9qnu3KRlh9b0KY?si=c450e5730fc64730). I'm excited for a future where teams can share and commit their sessions, so we can record how software gets built more faithfully than ever.
